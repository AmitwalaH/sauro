The IPCC's key message about 1.5°C of global warming is that the world is rapidly running out of time to limit global temperature rise to this level.

The important distinction is that 1.5°C is not a temperature at which Earth suddenly becomes uninhabitable. It is a threshold used to limit the severity of climate change. Every additional fraction of a degree increases the risks.

What does 1.5°C actually mean?
It means the Earth's average surface temperature rises 1.5°C above the pre-industrial average (roughly 1850–1900).

It does NOT mean that every location on Earth becomes just 1.5°C hotter. Some regions will experience much larger temperature increases.

1. The world is likely to reach 1.5°C in the 2030s
The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report (AR6), whose final synthesis was published in March 2023, found that:

Global warming is likely to reach 1.5°C in the near term (2021–2040).

Even very ambitious emissions reductions may not prevent a temporary crossing.

Under most assessed scenarios, the threshold is expected to be crossed around the early 2030s.

3. Is there still a chance to stay below 1.5°C?
The IPCC says limiting warming is still physically possible, but it requires immediate and substantial action.

Its proposed pathway includes:

Rapid and deep reductions in greenhouse gas emissions this decade.

Reaching global net-zero CO₂ emissions in the early 2050s.

Reducing dependence on fossil fuels.

Expanding renewable energy and improving energy efficiency.

The report also warns that exceeding 1.5°C temporarily and subsequently bringing temperatures down would require net-negative CO₂ emissions and could not reverse all the damage.

4. Is this still the latest IPCC report?
Yes. The Sixth Assessment Report's Synthesis Report (2023) remains the latest completed comprehensive IPCC assessment. The Seventh Assessment Cycle is underway, with major reports expected around 2028–2029.

The most important takeaway
1.5°C is not a point of no return for humanity. It is a critical climate limit beyond which risks become increasingly severe.

And one important scientific distinction: one individual year exceeding 1.5°C does not mean the long-term 1.5°C threshold has officially been crossed. The IPCC assesses this using a 20-year average.
